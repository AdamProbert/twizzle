4 · Wiring

Four parts, one cable

5 Vground signals (3.3 V)motor plug

check Some two-wire USB-C sockets leave out two tiny resistors that tell a USB-C charger "send 5 V". Without them, a C-to-C cable gives nothing, while an A-to-C cable works fine. Try yours with a C-to-C cable and a meter before it goes in the puck.

5 · Mount: twist and stack (chosen)

Twist and stack

  • Base plate stays stuck to the wall with two large Command strips. Strips hold well in shear, and this thing pulls mostly sideways.
  • Angle: the puck sits on a toothed ring on the base, so it clicks round in 5° steps. Twist to match the wand, done.
  • Distance: snap-in 5 mm spacer rings between base and puck (about five for your 50 mm gap). Measure the gap once, stack the right number.
  • Removable: the puck pops off the base, so you can slide the wand out or swap Command strips without fuss.
  • idea a slightly flared hex hole lets the wand sit a couple of degrees off without binding.
6 · Side profile, wand 50 mm off the wall

Floating puck, or one solid drum?

The wand's centre sits 17 mm into the puck (half the motor plus the back wall), so the puck's back has to be about 33 mm off the wall. Either way the whole thing sticks out about 68 mm. The question is what fills that 33 mm.

A · Floating my pick

  • A 90 mm puck on a 60 mm plinth. From the front it seems to float, with a soft shadow ring behind it.
  • Five 5 mm spacers. Add or remove one to fine-tune ±5 mm, or twist the whole puck for angle.
  • Keeps everything we've drawn so far.

B · Drum

  • Uses the 33 mm gap for the electronics: board and C3 go behind the motor.
  • Smaller face (~78 mm), but a chunkier, deeper body. More "can" than "puck".
  • Distance is fixed. Getting it right means a reprint or a shim.

The catch with the pot. A pot always knows where it's pointing. So if the dial means "blind position", it'll be wrong as soon as Google or Home Assistant moves the blind.

Better jobs for it:

  • "How open is open": set the slat angle the button and "Hey Google, open" go to. That setting never gets out of step.
  • Leave it out and keep the face cleaner. The setting could live in Home Assistant instead.
  • If you really want "turn to move", a rotary encoder (about £2, spins forever) is the right part, not a pot.

5 · Wiring (5 V only, now with dial)

Half the boards of round two

5 Vground signals (3.3 V)motor (5-wire plug)

The motor's red wire is its 5 V common, and the board switches the other four to ground in turn. In ESPHome this is the built-in uln2003 stepper with "sleep when done", so the coils go cold whenever the blind isn't moving. The pot sits across 3V3 and ground, with its middle leg read on GPIO1, which can measure voltages.

4 · Inside, roughly to scale (option 1)

Does it all fit?

  • Everything fits inside a ~115 mm puck, ~55 mm deep, so there's one unit and one USB-C cable coming out the bottom.
  • The NEMA sets the depth. A pancake NEMA 17 (~20 mm long, about £10) would get the puck to ~35 mm deep.
  • Your LM2596 is the bulkiest board. A thumbnail-sized mini buck (~£2) would free up a lot of room.
  • The wand hole runs off-centre so the motor fits. From the front the puck still looks round; only the hole sits left of middle.
5 · Wiring (options 1 and 2)

One USB-C cable in, everything inside the puck

The charger gives 12 V once the PD board asks for it. 12 V goes straight to the motor driver, and your LM2596 steps it down to 5 V for the ESP32-C3. Three signal wires tell the driver to step, which way, and when to switch off.
